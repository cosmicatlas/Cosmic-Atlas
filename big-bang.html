The Big Bang: The Beginning of Cosmic History

The universe is expanding and cooling. Run that backward and you reach a time when everything was hotter and denser than anything we can see today. That story is the Big Bang model.

The short version

The Big Bang model describes how the universe evolved from a hot, dense state into the cosmos we see now. Measurements of the expansion and of the oldest light in the sky give an age of about 13.8 billion years for the universe. Established theory

Important: the model describes the universe after that hot, dense state. It does not tell us what caused it, or whether anything came before. Open question

Three pieces of evidence

1. Distant galaxies are moving apart. The light from distant galaxies is stretched to longer wavelengths (redshift). On average, the farther the galaxy, the larger the redshift. Observed

The interpretation is that space itself is expanding, carrying galaxies apart like dots on an inflating balloon. This pattern is called the Hubble–Lemaître law. Established theory

2. The sky glows faintly in microwaves. A nearly uniform microwave glow comes from every direction, with a temperature of about 2.7 kelvin. It was first detected in 1965. Observed

This is the cosmic microwave background (CMB). It is interpreted as light released about 380,000 years after the hot, dense state, when the universe cooled enough for atoms to form and light could travel freely. Tiny variations of roughly one part in 100,000 in the CMB map the seeds of later galaxies. Established theory

3. The first elements match predictions. For a few minutes the early universe was hot enough for nuclear fusion. Calculations predict about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium-4 by mass, plus tiny traces of deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7. Measurements of primordial gas agree well for helium-4 and deuterium. Observed

One mismatch: stars with very little heavy-element content show less lithium-7 than the calculations predict. Scientists have not settled why. Open question

A timeline of the early universe

  1. The earliest instantsOur known physics cannot describe these moments. Ideas such as cosmic inflation try to, but none is confirmed. Hypothesis
  2. Within the first secondA hot soup of particles and radiation fills space, and protons and neutrons form. Established theory
  3. A few minutesNuclear fusion builds the light nuclei. Established theory
  4. About 380,000 yearsAtoms form and the CMB light is released. Observed
  5. The first few hundred million yearsThe first stars and galaxies appear. Their exact timing is still being studied. Open question
  6. About 13.8 billion yearsToday.

What the Big Bang model does not say

It was not an explosion in space. Space itself expanded everywhere at once. There is no center that galaxies fly away from.

It does not describe a "point." If you run the equations of general relativity backward all the way, they give infinite density, called a singularity. Physicists treat that as a sign that the theory stops working there, not as something observed.

It does not explain its own cause. It describes how a hot, dense universe evolved. It is silent about what, if anything, came before.

What we still don't know

What happened at the very beginning. Cosmic inflation is a leading hypothesis for the earliest moments, but its precise mechanism is uncertain and alternatives exist. Hypothesis

What dark matter and dark energy are. Both are needed in the standard model to match observations, but their nature is unknown. Open question

The Hubble tension. Measuring the expansion rate from the early-universe CMB and from nearby galaxies gives values that differ by several percent. Whether this points to new physics or measurement issues is an active debate. Open question

Check your understanding

Did the Big Bang happen at one point in space?

No. The model describes the expansion of space itself, everywhere. There is no center.

Name one observation that supports a hot early universe.

The CMB, the redshifts of distant galaxies, or the measured amounts of hydrogen and helium.

Is cosmic inflation as well established as the CMB?

No. The CMB is a direct observation. Inflation is a leading hypothesis that is still being tested.
